## 1. Idea and Opportunity
**Lecture:** 3 - Identifying and Evaluating Opportunities

### Simple Meaning
- **Idea:** A thought, concept, or suggestion that may solve a problem or satisfy a need.
- **Opportunity:** A real chance to turn that idea into a successful and profitable business.

**Easy line to remember:**
An idea is a thought. An opportunity is a market-ready chance.

### Difference Between Idea and Opportunity

| Aspect | Idea | Opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A creative thought or concept | A chance to turn the idea into a viable business |
| Focus | Solving a problem or meeting a need | Creating value, demand, and profit |
| Market demand | May or may not have demand | Must have market demand |
| Timing | Can come at any time | Needs the right time and market conditions |
| Profit potential | Not always clear | Has income and value potential |
| Viability | May not be practical | More likely to be practical and profitable |
| Link with goals | Can come from anywhere | Should match the entrepreneur's goals and vision |

### Full Explanation
- Every business starts with an **idea**, but not every idea becomes a business.
- An idea becomes an **opportunity** only when there is:
  - customer need,
  - right timing,
  - market gap,
  - profit potential,
  - and the possibility of creating value.

### Four Essential Qualities of an Opportunity
An opportunity should be:

1. **Attractive**
   It should serve a market big enough to earn profit.

2. **Timely**
   It should be needed now, not too early or too late.

3. **Durable**
   It should continue for some time and not disappear quickly like a fad.

4. **Unique**
   It should create or add value for customers in a distinct way.

**Easy memory trick:** `ATDU`
- Attractive
- Timely
- Durable
- Unique

### Three Ways to Identify an Opportunity

#### 1. Observing Trends
Entrepreneurs watch changes happening around them because trends often create business opportunities.

The four important trends are:

- **Economic forces**
  - Show where startups should enter or avoid.
  - Example: In a weak economy, people want to save money, so money-saving businesses can grow.
  - Example from lecture: `GasBuddy.com`.

- **Social forces**
  - Change people's behavior and priorities.
  - Examples:
    - aging population,
    - more workplace diversity,
    - social network use,
    - more mobile device use,
    - greater focus on health and wellness.

- **Technological advances**
  - New technology creates new industries and products.
  - Examples:
    - computer industry,
    - internet,
    - biotechnology,
    - digital photography.
  - Example from lecture: `H20Audio`, which created waterproof accessories for iPhones.

- **Political and regulatory changes**
  - New laws and rules create new needs.
  - Example: Environmental laws create opportunities for firms that help others follow regulations.

#### 2. Solving a Problem
- Sometimes an opportunity is found simply by noticing a problem and solving it.
- Many companies start because founders personally experienced a problem and then created a solution.
- Problems may be discovered through:
  - trends,
  - intuition,
  - chance,
  - change in daily life.

#### 3. Finding Gaps in the Marketplace
- A market gap exists when a group of people needs a product or service that is not properly served.
- Mainstream companies may ignore that group because the segment looks too small or too specialized.
- For entrepreneurs, that ignored need can become a strong business opportunity.

### Personal Characteristics That Help Opportunity Recognition
Some people are better at recognizing opportunities because of the following:

#### 1. Prior Experience
- Work experience helps a person understand an industry deeply.
- It helps in spotting unmet customer needs and underserved market niches.
- It also helps in building useful contacts.

#### 2. Cognitive Factors
- Some people have strong **entrepreneurial alertness**.
- This means they notice useful opportunities without even doing a formal search.
- It is like a "business sixth sense."

#### 3. Social Networks
- People with strong networks hear more ideas and see more opportunities.
- Research in the lecture says many people got their business idea through social contacts.

Two types of relationships:

- **Strong-tie relationships**
  - Close friends, family, and regular contacts.
  - These usually reinforce what we already know.

- **Weak-tie relationships**
  - Casual contacts or acquaintances.
  - These often bring fresh ideas and new perspectives.

**Important point:** Weak-tie relationships often produce more new business ideas than strong-tie relationships.

#### 4. Creativity
- Creativity means generating a new or useful idea.
- Opportunity recognition is partly a creative process.

### Five Stages of the Creative Process

1. **Preparation**
   - Background, knowledge, and experience.
   - The entrepreneur gathers information and understands the field.

2. **Incubation**
   - The person keeps thinking about the problem.
   - Ideas "cook" in the mind, sometimes consciously and sometimes unconsciously.

3. **Insight**
   - The "eureka" moment.
   - This is when the idea or opportunity becomes clear.

4. **Evaluation**
   - The idea is checked carefully for viability.
   - This is very important because many people skip this step and rush too early.

5. **Elaboration**
   - The idea is shaped into a final form.
   - This is where details are worked out and the idea becomes a product, service, or business concept.
   - In a new business, this is the stage where the **business plan** is written.

### Techniques for Generating Ideas

#### 1. Eidetic Image Method
- "Eidetic" means seeing an image in great detail.
- In brainstorming, people imagine the problem or product clearly and use that mental image to generate ideas.
- This helps create creative and specific solutions.

#### 2. Starbursting Method
- The main idea is placed in the center of a star.
- Around it, ask:
  - Who?
  - What?
  - Where?
  - When?
  - Why?
  - How?
- This method helps examine an idea from all angles.

#### 3. SCAMPER Method
SCAMPER helps improve or create products and services by asking seven types of questions:

- **S - Substitute**
  What can be replaced?

- **C - Combine**
  What can be combined?

- **A - Adapt**
  What can be added or adjusted from another idea?

- **M - Modify / Magnify / Minify**
  What can be changed in size, shape, color, or performance?

- **P - Put to another use**
  Can it be used in another way or another industry?

- **E - Eliminate**
  What can be removed or simplified?

- **R - Reverse / Rearrange / Reengineer**
  What can be flipped, reversed, or reordered?

### Short Exam Summary
- An **idea** is a concept.
- An **opportunity** is a valuable and profitable business possibility.
- A good opportunity is attractive, timely, durable, and unique.
- Opportunities can be found by observing trends, solving problems, and finding market gaps.

## 2. Myths About Entrepreneurship
**Lecture:** 2 - Entrepreneurial Mindset

Entrepreneurship is often misunderstood. These misunderstandings are called myths.

### Myth 1: Entrepreneurs are born, not made
**Reality:** Entrepreneurial skills can be developed.

- People are not automatically successful just because they are born with talent.
- Skills like leadership, communication, market analysis, decision-making, and risk management can be learned.
- Education, experience, and practice help a person become entrepreneurial.

### Myth 2: Entrepreneurs are gamblers
**Reality:** Entrepreneurs take calculated risks, not blind risks.

- Gamblers depend mostly on luck.
- Entrepreneurs study the situation, estimate possible outcomes, and then take a planned risk.
- They try to reduce uncertainty through research and testing.

### Myth 3: Entrepreneurs work alone
**Reality:** Successful entrepreneurs depend on teams, contacts, mentors, and networks.

- No entrepreneur can do everything alone.
- They need:
  - skilled employees,
  - partners,
  - advisors,
  - investors,
  - suppliers,
  - and customers.
- Team building is a major part of success.

### Myth 4: A good idea is enough
**Reality:** A good idea alone is not enough.

- An idea must also have:
  - market demand,
  - proper timing,
  - good execution,
  - customer value,
  - and a workable business model.
- Many good ideas fail because execution is poor.

### Myth 5: Success comes quickly
**Reality:** Entrepreneurship takes time, effort, setbacks, and persistence.

- Startups usually face:
  - mistakes,
  - competition,
  - delays,
  - financial pressure,
  - and learning curves.
- Real success often comes after repeated improvement and patience.

### Why These Myths Matter
- These myths create unrealistic expectations.
- They can discourage students and new entrepreneurs.
- Understanding the truth helps people prepare in a practical and confident way.

### Short Exam Summary
- Entrepreneurs are **made through learning and experience**.
- They take **calculated risks**, not blind risks.
- They **do not work alone**.
- A good idea must be supported by **execution and demand**.
- Success needs **time, learning, and persistence**.

## 3. Feasibility Analysis
**Lecture:** 4 - Feasibility Analysis

### What is Feasibility Analysis?
Feasibility analysis is the process of checking whether a business idea is practical and worth pursuing.

It is:
- a **preliminary evaluation**,
- done **before writing a business plan**,
- used to reduce guesswork,
- and used to judge whether an idea is **viable**.

### Timing of Feasibility Analysis
The sequence is:

1. Recognize an opportunity
2. Conduct feasibility analysis
3. Prepare the business plan

**Easy line to remember:**  
Opportunity first, feasibility second, business plan third.

### Why Feasibility Analysis is Important
- It helps avoid wasting time and money on weak ideas.
- It gives a clearer picture of whether the business can succeed.
- It highlights strengths, risks, requirements, and possible problems early.
- It increases confidence before moving toward launch.

### Four Forms of Feasibility Analysis

1. Product/Service Feasibility
2. Industry/Target Market Feasibility
3. Organizational Feasibility
4. Financial Feasibility

**Easy memory trick:** `P-I-O-F`

### 1. Product/Service Feasibility
This checks whether the proposed product or service is realistic and likely to succeed in the market.

It looks at:
- market demand,
- competition,
- regulatory considerations,
- technical feasibility,
- and practical success potential.

#### Key Questions
- Do customers really want it?
- Is there enough demand?
- Are competitors already strong?
- Can the product be developed properly?
- Can legal and technical requirements be met?

#### Example from Lecture: XYZ HealthTech
XYZ HealthTech wanted to launch a telemedicine platform for online medical consultations.

To test feasibility, the company examined:

- **Market Demand**
  - Are patients and doctors interested?
  - What do demographics and healthcare trends show?
  - Will users adopt telemedicine?

- **Competition**
  - Who already offers similar services?
  - What are their strengths, weaknesses, pricing, and market share?
  - How can the new platform be different?

- **Regulatory Considerations**
  - Does the product follow laws and ethical standards?
  - Patient privacy
  - data security
  - medical licensing
  - telehealth regulations

- **Technical Feasibility**
  - Can the company build and maintain the platform?
  - Does it have the software, IT infrastructure, compatibility, scalability, and cybersecurity needed?

### 2. Industry/Target Market Feasibility
This checks the attractiveness of the industry and the specific market segment the business wants to serve.

#### Difference Between Industry and Target Market
- **Industry:** All firms making similar products or services.
- **Target market:** The specific group of customers the business wants to serve.

**Important point:** Startups usually do not target the whole industry. They select a clear niche.

#### Two Components

##### A. Industry Attractiveness
This means checking whether the industry itself is worth entering.

Important factors:
- market size,
- growth rate,
- competition,
- barriers to entry,
- supplier power,
- buyer power,
- technological trends,
- regulatory environment,
- profitability.

**Example:** The electric vehicle industry is attractive because of:
- rising demand,
- government support,
- better battery technology,
- and increasing environmental awareness.

But it also has challenges such as:
- high capital needs,
- regulations,
- and competition from large companies.

##### B. Target Market Attractiveness
This means checking whether the chosen customer segment is worth targeting.

Important factors:
- segment size,
- growth potential,
- purchasing power,
- accessibility,
- needs and preferences,
- behavioral patterns,
- competition,
- alignment with company strengths.

**Example:** In the smartphone market, a company may target youth because:
- the segment is large,
- tech adoption is high,
- and purchasing power is improving.

Another lecture example in EVs:
- environmentally conscious customers,
- willing to pay more for sustainable vehicles,
- interested in long battery life and fast charging.

### 3. Organizational Feasibility
This checks whether the business has the internal ability to launch and run successfully.

It focuses on whether the firm has:
- strong management,
- the right structure,
- and enough skilled people and resources.

#### Main Factors

- **Management Expertise**
  - Does the management team know the market, product, and strategy?

- **Organizational Structure**
  - Can the current structure handle the new business or expansion?
  - Are roles, departments, and decision-making clear?

- **Human Resources**
  - Are enough skilled workers available?
  - Are training, hiring, or restructuring needed?

#### Example from Lecture
XYZ Corporation wanted to expand from automotive parts into electric vehicle components.
It checked:
- whether management had EV knowledge,
- whether the structure could support the new line,
- and whether staff had the right skills.

### 4. Financial Feasibility
This checks whether the business idea makes financial sense.

It examines whether the expected returns are enough to justify the investment.

#### Main Factors

- **Revenue Projections**
  - How much money can the business earn?

- **Cost Estimates**
  - What will it cost to develop, launch, and run?

- **Funding Requirements**
  - How much money is needed?
  - Where will funding come from?

- **Cash Flow Projections**
  - When will cash come in and go out?
  - Can the firm pay its bills on time?

- **Return on Investment (ROI)**
  - Is the project worth the investment?
  - Extra tools mentioned in lecture: payback period, NPV, IRR.

#### Example from Lecture: ABC Tech
ABC Tech wanted to build a fitness mobile app.

It studied:
- future app revenue,
- development and marketing costs,
- total funding needed,
- cash inflow and outflow timing,
- and expected return on investment.

### Role of Feasibility Analysis in Business Success
- It acts as a filter before major investment.
- It helps separate strong ideas from weak ideas.
- It supports smarter decision-making.
- It reduces uncertainty and business failure risk.

### Short Exam Summary
- Feasibility analysis checks whether a business idea is worth pursuing.
- It comes **after opportunity recognition** and **before the business plan**.
- It has four forms:
  - product/service,
  - industry/target market,
  - organizational,
  - financial.

## 4. Build-Measure-Learn Cycle
**Lecture:** 6 - Lean Startup Methodology

### Lean Startup Meaning
The Lean Startup methodology was developed by **Eric Ries**.

It is a way of building startups through:
- continuous innovation,
- validated learning,
- and rapid iteration.

### What is the Build-Measure-Learn Cycle?
The Build-Measure-Learn cycle is the core cycle of the Lean Startup approach.

Instead of building a full product first and hoping it works, the startup:

1. Builds a basic version
2. Measures how users respond
3. Learns from the results
4. Improves, repeats, or changes direction

### The Three Steps

#### 1. Build
- Create a simple version of the product.
- This simple version is usually called the **MVP**.

**MVP = Minimum Viable Product**
- The simplest version of a product
- Built with least effort
- Used to get maximum learning from customers

The goal is not perfection.
The goal is learning.

#### 2. Measure
- Collect real data from users and the market.
- Check how people respond to the MVP.

Things that may be measured:
- user sign-ups,
- customer feedback,
- sales,
- usage frequency,
- retention,
- interest level,
- adoption rate.

#### 3. Learn
- Understand what the data means.
- Check whether assumptions were right or wrong.
- Learn what customers actually want.

After learning, the startup decides:

- **Persevere**
  Continue with the same strategy because the idea is working.

- **Pivot**
  Change an important part of the strategy because the current approach is not working.

### Easy Formula
Build small -> Measure real -> Learn fast -> Improve or pivot

### Why This Cycle is Important
- Saves time and money
- Reduces risk
- Gives real customer feedback early
- Prevents building products nobody wants
- Supports fast improvement

### Related Lean Startup Principles

#### 1. Validated Learning
- Learning should come from experiments and real data, not assumptions.

**Example:** Airbnb founders tested whether people would rent space in homes by offering air mattresses during a conference.

#### 2. Minimum Viable Product (MVP)
- The minimum version needed to test the business idea with customers.

**Example:** Tesla's Roadster acted as an early MVP before larger expansion.

#### 3. Continuous Deployment
- Release improvements frequently and collect new feedback continuously.

**Example:** Facebook regularly updates features based on user data.

#### 4. Pivot or Persevere
- If results are weak, pivot.
- If results are strong, continue.

**Example:** Instagram started as `Burbn`, a location-based app, and then pivoted to photo sharing.

#### 5. Fail Fast, Fail Cheap
- Test quickly and cheaply.
- Treat failure as learning, not disaster.

### Minimum Viable Product (MVP)
- A **minimum viable product** is the version of a new product that allows a team to collect the **maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort**.
- In simple words, it is the smallest workable version of a product used for testing.
- Its purpose is not to be perfect.
- Its purpose is to learn what customers actually want.

**Important lecture point:**
- The MVP works through the **Build-Measure-Learn** cycle.
- This cycle is the foundation of Lean Startup methodology.

### Product/Market Fit vs Problem/Solution Fit

#### Traditional Product/Market Fit
- Build the product first
- Then test it again and again to find market fit
- In this approach, the entrepreneur starts by building something and then tries to discover whether the market really wants it

**Simple flow:**  
Build -> Demo -> Sell

#### Meaning of Product/Market Fit
- Product/market fit means the product satisfies a real market need.
- It means the business has created something people actually want, use, and are willing to pay for.

#### Lean Startup Problem/Solution Fit or Market First
- Validate demand first
- Then build more confidently
- Start by understanding the **problem**, not by falling in love with the solution
- Create an offer or demo that can be sold before the full product is built

Lecture idea:
- Build a demo first
- Try to sell the demo
- If people want it, then invest more in building the full product

**Simple flow:**  
Demo -> Sell -> Build

### Market First
Market First means:

- first understand the customer's problem,
- then create a demo or simple offer,
- then test whether people are willing to buy,
- and only after validation, build the full product.

This approach reduces waste because the business does not spend too much time and money building something the market may reject.

### Core Difference Between the Two Approaches

| Approach | Starting Point | Flow | Main Idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product/Market Fit | Product | Build -> Demo -> Sell | Build first, then search for the market |
| Problem/Solution Fit / Market First | Problem | Demo -> Sell -> Build | Validate the market first, then build |

### Very Important Exam Point
- In **product/market fit**, entrepreneurs may become too attached to the product or solution first.
- In **problem/solution fit**, entrepreneurs focus first on the customer's problem.
- Lean Startup prefers starting with the **problem**, testing a **demo**, and checking if it can be **sold** before full development.

**Example:** Tesla often demonstrates and pre-sells cars before large-scale production.

### How MVP Connects to Product/Market Fit
- A startup may have many possible product ideas.
- The market has real needs and demands.
- The **MVP** is used to test whether one of those product ideas actually fits the market.
- When the MVP satisfies a real need, the startup moves closer to **product/market fit**.

### Famous MVP Examples from Lecture

- **Airbnb**
  - MVP: renting out air mattresses and offering breakfast
  - Learning: people were willing to pay for temporary accommodation

- **Dropbox**
  - MVP: a simple video demo
  - Learning: users were interested before the full product was built

- **Facebook**
  - MVP: network only for Harvard students
  - Learning: the model could expand to other universities and then globally

- **Twitter**
  - MVP: tested internally at Odeo

- **Slack**
  - MVP: internal tool at Tiny Speck
  - Later pivoted into a successful business communication platform

- **YouTube**
  - MVP: first test video upload

- **WhatsApp**
  - MVP: small group messaging test

- **LinkedIn**
  - MVP: launched to colleagues and friends first

- **Uber**
  - MVP: simple black-car app used by a small group

- **Spotify**
  - MVP: launched in Sweden with a limited catalog

### Short Exam Summary
- Build-Measure-Learn is the heart of Lean Startup.
- Build a simple product, measure customer response, and learn from real data.
- Use MVPs, validated learning, and quick iteration.
- MVP means collecting maximum customer learning with minimum effort.
- Market First means validate the market before fully building the product.
- Problem/solution fit starts with the problem, while product/market fit often starts with the product.
- At the end of learning, decide to **pivot or persevere**.

## 5. Porter's Five Forces
**Lecture:** 7 - Competitive Analysis

### Introduction
Porter's Five Forces model was created by **Michael Porter** and first published in **1979**.

It is a framework used to study the competitive pressure inside an industry.

It helps businesses understand:
- how attractive an industry is,
- how strong competition is,
- and how easy or difficult it will be to earn profit.

### Main Idea
According to Porter, five forces shape industry competition and profitability.

If these forces are strong, profitability usually becomes lower.
If these forces are weak, profitability is usually better.

### The Five Forces

#### 1. Threat of New Entrants
This means how easy it is for new companies to enter the industry.

If entry is easy:
- competition increases,
- prices may fall,
- profits may fall.

If entry is difficult:
- existing firms are safer,
- profits may remain stronger.

Factors affecting this force:
- high capital requirements,
- brand loyalty,
- economies of scale,
- government regulations,
- access to distribution,
- infrastructure needs.

**Lecture example:** Airline industry
- High capital requirement
- many regulations
- infrastructure is expensive
- so new entry is difficult

#### 2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers
This means how much power suppliers have over businesses.

If suppliers are powerful, they can:
- charge higher prices,
- reduce quality,
- limit supply,
- create pressure on profits.

Supplier power is high when:
- suppliers are few,
- inputs are unique,
- switching suppliers is hard,
- the business depends heavily on them.

**Lecture example:** Smartphone industry
- Companies depend on components like processors and displays.
- Supplier relationships strongly affect quality and pricing.

#### 3. Bargaining Power of Buyers
This means how much power customers have over businesses.

If buyers are powerful, they can:
- demand lower prices,
- ask for better quality,
- easily switch to other sellers.

Buyer power is high when:
- many alternatives exist,
- price comparison is easy,
- switching cost is low,
- customers are well informed.

**Lecture example:** Retail industry
- Customers can compare prices online very easily.
- So companies must keep prices competitive and improve value.

#### 4. Threat of Substitute Products or Services
This means the danger that another product may satisfy the same need in a different way.

Substitutes reduce demand and limit pricing power.

Substitute threat is high when:
- alternatives are available,
- alternatives are convenient,
- alternatives are cheaper or better.

**Lecture example:** Music industry
- Streaming services like Spotify and Apple Music became substitutes for CDs and downloads.

#### 5. Intensity of Competitive Rivalry
This means how strongly existing firms compete against each other.

Rivalry is high when:
- many strong competitors exist,
- growth is slow,
- products are similar,
- companies fight through advertising, pricing, and innovation,
- exit barriers are high.

**Lecture example:** Soft drink industry
- Coca-Cola and Pepsi constantly compete for market share.

### Why Porter's Five Forces is Useful
- Helps in industry analysis
- Helps in strategy making
- Helps in identifying risks
- Helps in checking future profitability
- Helps startups understand where pressure will come from

### Easy Way to Remember the Five Forces
Remember these five:

1. New entrants
2. Suppliers
3. Buyers
4. Substitutes
5. Rivalry

### Short Exam Summary
- Porter's Five Forces explains the competitive pressures in an industry.
- The five forces are:
  - threat of new entrants,
  - bargaining power of suppliers,
  - bargaining power of buyers,
  - threat of substitutes,
  - intensity of rivalry.
- Stronger forces usually mean lower profits.

## 6. Business Plan
**Lecture:** 8 - Developing a Business Plan

### What is a Business Plan?
A business plan is a comprehensive written document that explains a business idea and how the business will operate, grow, and succeed.

It includes:
- goals,
- strategies,
- operations,
- financial details,
- and plans for the future.

### Why a Business Plan is Important
- It acts as a roadmap.
- It helps entrepreneurs think clearly.
- It helps attract investors, lenders, and partners.
- It ensures that important parts of the business are planned carefully.
- It turns a business idea into a structured action plan.

### Nine Main Parts of a Business Plan

#### 1. Executive Summary
- Short overview of the whole business plan
- Includes the concept, goals, and major highlights

#### 2. Company Description
- Mission
- vision
- legal structure
- location
- business background

#### 3. Market Analysis
- Industry research
- target market
- customer demographics
- competition
- trends

#### 4. Organization and Management
- Organizational structure
- key people
- roles and responsibilities

#### 5. Product or Service Line
- What the business offers
- features
- benefits
- unique selling points

#### 6. Marketing and Sales Strategy
- How customers will be reached
- pricing
- promotion
- distribution
- sales methods

#### 7. Funding Request
- How much money is needed
- where it will be used
- possible sources of finance

#### 8. Financial Projections
- Expected financial performance
- income statements
- cash flow projections
- balance sheets
- profitability estimates

#### 9. Appendix
- Supporting documents
- resumes
- research data
- legal papers
- technical details

### Easy Way to Remember the Structure
Think in three blocks:

#### Block 1: Business Basics
- Executive Summary
- Company Description
- Market Analysis

#### Block 2: How the Business Works
- Organization and Management
- Product or Service Line
- Marketing and Sales Strategy

#### Block 3: Money and Proof
- Funding Request
- Financial Projections
- Appendix

### Business Plan vs Business Model

| Aspect | Business Plan | Business Model |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Full written document | Framework for how value is created, delivered, and captured |
| Focus | Overall strategy, operations, and finance | Core logic of making money and sustaining the business |
| Nature | More detailed and formal | More conceptual and flexible |
| Main question | How will the business run and grow? | How will the business create and capture value? |
| Audience | Mostly investors, lenders, stakeholders | Internal and external stakeholders |
| Change | Relatively static, updated when needed | More dynamic and adaptable |

**Simple difference:**
- **Business plan** = full roadmap
- **Business model** = how the business makes money

### Presenting the Business Plan to Investors
- If investors are interested, founders usually present the plan in person.
- Founders should create a strong and professional impression.
- The business plan is not only for writing; it must also be presented clearly.

### Oral Presentation Rules
- Follow instructions exactly.
- If given 1 hour with 20 minutes presentation and 40 minutes Q&A, do not exceed 20 minutes for the presentation.
- Presentation should be:
  - smooth,
  - well-rehearsed,
  - clear,
  - and not cluttered.

### Investor Slides
- The lecture/image includes **twelve PowerPoint slides** for an investor presentation.
- These slides help founders present the business clearly, briefly, and professionally.

#### Twelve PowerPoint Slides to Include in an Investor Presentation

1. **Title Slide**
   - Introduce the presentation with the company name.
   - Include the founders' names.
   - If available, include the company logo.

2. **Problem**
   - Briefly explain the problem to be solved or the need to be filled.

3. **Solution**
   - Explain how the business will solve the problem.
   - Show how the product or service will satisfy the need.

4. **Opportunity and Target Market**
   - Describe the specific target market.
   - Explain the business and environmental trends creating the market opportunity.

5. **Technology**
   - This slide is optional but usually included.
   - Explain the technology or any unusual aspect of the product or service.
   - Do not explain it in an overly technical way.
   - Keep the description simple and interesting.

6. **Competition**
   - Explain the firm's competitive advantage in the marketplace.
   - Show how the firm will compete against established competitors.

7. **Marketing and Sales**
   - Describe the overall marketing strategy.
   - Explain the sales process.
   - If primary research was done, mention what people felt about the product and report the results here.

8. **Management Team**
   - Describe the current management team.
   - Explain how the team came together.
   - Show why their backgrounds and experience qualify them to succeed.
   - If there is a board of advisors or board of directors, briefly mention the key individuals.
   - If there are gaps in the team, explain how and when they will be filled.

9. **Financial Projections**
   - Briefly discuss the financials.
   - Explain how the firm will achieve profitability.
   - Mention how much capital is needed.
   - Explain when cash flow will break even.
   - Use extra slides only if absolutely needed to present financial information properly.

10. **Current Status**
   - Describe the current stage of the venture.
   - Present it in the context of the milestones achieved so far.
   - Do not reduce the importance of what has already been accomplished.

11. **Financing Sought**
   - State clearly how much financing is being requested.
   - Explain exactly how that money will be used.

12. **Summary**
   - Close the presentation strongly.
   - Summarize the strongest points of the venture and the team.
   - Invite questions and feedback from the audience.

### Easy Way to Remember the 12 Slides
- Start with the business identity: title
- Then explain the market logic: problem, solution, opportunity
- Then explain the business strength: technology, competition, marketing
- Then explain the people and money: management, financials, current status, financing
- End with a strong summary

### Short Exam Summary
- A business plan is a detailed roadmap for starting and running a business.
- It contains 9 main parts.
- It helps communicate the business idea to investors and other stakeholders.
- It is different from a business model, which explains how value and revenue are created.

### Idea vs Opportunity
- Idea = concept
- Opportunity = profitable market chance
- Good opportunity = attractive, timely, durable, unique

### Myths
- Entrepreneurs are not only born
- They are not gamblers
- They do not work alone
- A good idea alone is not enough
- Success is not instant

### Feasibility Analysis
- Done before business plan
- Checks if idea is worth pursuing
- Four forms: product/service, industry/target market, organizational, financial

### Build-Measure-Learn
- Build MVP
- Measure response
- Learn from data
- Pivot or persevere

### Porter's Five Forces
- New entrants
- Supplier power
- Buyer power
- Substitutes
- Rivalry

### Business Plan
- Detailed roadmap of the business
- 9 parts
- Used to guide business and attract investors
